THE REWATCH-O-METER

A question Hollywood accounting never asks: if zero new people had shown up, how many more times would everyone who did see it need to buy another ticket — just to drag the thing to breakeven?

METHOD: breakeven ≈ 2.2× budget (industry rule of thumb). Shortfall ÷ $10 assumed ticket price = extra tickets needed. Divided by estimated original audience (gross ÷ $10) = the index.

MEASURED IN PIZZA

New York has tracked its own economy in pizza for 45+ years. The "Pizza Principle" is a real, documented phenomenon: since the early 1960s, the price of a plain NYC slice has matched the subway fare with uncanny precision — both journalists and a Columbia statistician have tracked it for decades. So here's the same logic, pointed at Hollywood instead of the MTA: convert each flop's loss into slices, priced the year it actually happened.

METHOD: same nominal shortfall used in the Rewatch-O-Meter, divided by that year's average NYC plain-slice price. 1995/2005/2024 prices are interpolated between documented years; all others are directly sourced.

RE-RANKED BY THE SLICE

Dollar-loss order and slice-count order are not the same list — because a slice cost 90¢ in 1980 and $3.81 in 2025, the same loss buys wildly different amounts of pizza depending on when it happened.

HOW A FLOP IS MADE

Industry rule of thumb: a wide release needs 2 to 2.5× its budget back just to break even, once marketing and the exhibitor's cut are covered.

THE 3 USUAL SUSPECTS

The budget ran wild. John Carter's marketing + production reportedly topped $350M before a ticket sold.

Nobody wanted it, loudly. Cutthroat Island, Mars Needs Moms, and Digger all paired bad reviews with audience shrugs.

Wrong week, wrong year. Sinbad and Mortal Engines both opened right before Aquaman and Mary Poppins Returns ate their lunch.

THE TREND NOBODY EXPECTED: SCARES ARE DOWN, NOT UP

1981
peak year (The Evil Dead: 1 scare every 4 min)
↓
declining since, despite more horror films being made

Popular assumption: horror keeps escalating. The data says otherwise — jump-scare density peaked around 1981 and has been on a genuine decline since, even as horror output has grown. One proposed cause: the rise of "elevated horror" (character- and visual-driven, not scare-driven) starting in the mid-2010s.

Source: Washington Post Department of Data analysis, using WheresTheJump.com's tracked-film database.

REMAKES: A SYSTEMATIC, RECENT DECLINE

Of 34 remake/original pairs studied, only 6 remakes had fewer scares than their source film. 5 of those 6 were made in the last decade — and every single remake released since 2020 has had fewer scares than its original.

Source: Washington Post Department of Data.

WHAT A "MAJOR" SCARE EVEN MEANS

Both trackers split scares into "major" and "minor" — but the ratio each assigns to the same film varies enormously. Severity classification is even less standardized than the raw count.

03THE ALL-TIME LEADERBOARD, BY DECADE

The record holder, "The Haunting in Connecticut 2" (2013), logged 32 jump scares — more than 5x the count in "Halloween" (1978). This is the closest thing horror has to an inflation index. The tracker's own database only has one entry that qualifies for the 1980s top list — coverage gaps are real and shown, not smoothed over.

Source: WheresTheJump.com, "Horror Movies With the Most Jump Scares."

Flanagan's "The Midnight Club" premiere holds the official Guinness World Record for most jump scares in a single TV episode — more than an entire season of his own "Haunting of Bly Manor" (16 total) or "Midnight Mass" (11 total).

Source: Guinness World Records; Deadline interview with Mike Flanagan.

04JUMP SCARES PER FILM, BY FRANCHISE VILLAIN

Michael Myers (Halloween) averages 11 jump scares per appearance, alongside an average 18 on-screen kills — the highest combined "villain effectiveness score" (8.71/10) in a dedicated franchise study.

Source: Euronews / industry study on horror villain effectiveness, Oct 2022.

A COMPLETELY DIFFERENT METHOD: HEART RATE, NOT HEAD COUNT

One study skipped manual counting entirely and monitored real viewers' heart rates instead. By that method, "Insidious" (2010) recorded a +23.1 BPM average increase over resting heart rate — the highest tension score in the study, using a completely different methodology than any manual jump-scare count on this page.

Source: Atom Tickets biometric horror-film study.

Methodology note

No governing body defines what counts as a "jump scare." Every number in this report comes from a specific named source using its own criteria (manual review, community submission, or biometric monitoring). Where two sources disagree, both are shown rather than reconciled into a single false-precise figure. This report treats jump scares as a genuine, if informally measured, cultural data point — same as any other proxy metric.
